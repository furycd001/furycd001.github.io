The Gentoo Linux community is facing a small but revealing reminder of one of the biggest challenges in open-source software: keeping a project alive is not always as simple as making the source code available.

On 24 October 2026, Gentoo is scheduled to remove Chromium from its official repository unless someone takes responsibility for maintaining it. The decision follows mounting difficulties keeping the browser updated, testing new releases and managing the enormous amount of work involved in building it from source. Reports indicate that compilation can take up to 16 hours on a laptop, while ongoing maintenance demands several hours of work each week.

The irony is that Chromium is open-source software, yet that does not make it effortless to maintain. Its rapid release cycle, complex dependencies and security requirements create a considerable burden for anyone volunteering to package it for a distribution such as Gentoo.

Fortunately, this does not mean Linux users are losing access to Chromium-based browsers. Google Chrome remains available as a precompiled package, alongside alternatives such as Firefox, Vivaldi and Microsoft Edge. Community-maintained Chromium builds are also available through Flatpak repositories such as Flathub.

But the more interesting story goes beyond one browser or one Linux distribution.

Open-source software depends on people willing to do the often invisible work of maintaining, testing, documenting and updating the tools that millions of others rely upon. A project can have millions of users and still struggle to find someone willing to dedicate their time to keeping a particular package working.

**Making software free to use is one thing; making it sustainable to maintain is another.**

Gentoo's decision is a reminder that open-source software is not maintained by good intentions alone. It requires time, expertise and people who are willing and able to keep showing up. When that commitment becomes unsustainable, even widely used software can disappear from an official repository.

Perhaps the lesson is that we should appreciate not only the software we use, but also the people quietly doing the work that allows us to keep using it.
