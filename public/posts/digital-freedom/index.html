A Small Attempt at Digital Freedom

July 21, 2026

I’ve been thinking lately about the files I create and the formats I use to store them.

On Linux, I’ve always appreciated the feeling of freedom that comes with being able to choose my own path. I can install what I want, configure things the way I like, and use programs that don’t necessarily belong to one particular company or ecosystem.

But I’ve started wondering about something else.

What happens to the files I create?

It is one thing to use open-source software. It is another to make sure that the things created with that software remain accessible long after the software itself has disappeared.

So I’ve been trying, where practical, to use as many open file formats as I can.

For documents, I might choose plain text or Markdown. For images, PNG or JPEG. For audio, perhaps FLAC or Ogg Vorbis. For video, formats such as WebM or Matroska. There are plenty of choices, and I don’t pretend that every format I use is perfectly open or ideal in every situation.

The point isn’t perfection.

The point is awareness.

I don’t want to create something today only to discover years from now that I need to find an old piece of software, from a company that may no longer exist, just to open something I created myself.

There is something comforting about a plain text file.

It doesn’t need much. It doesn’t care what operating system I’m using. It doesn’t demand a subscription. It doesn’t need a particular application to understand it. It is just text.

I can open it in a terminal, in a text editor, in a browser, or in some piece of software that hasn’t been updated in years.

That feels important to me.

Computers are supposed to be tools for creating and storing our thoughts, our photographs, our music, our memories and our work. I like the idea that those things should belong to me rather than being permanently tied to the software I happened to use at the time.

Of course, the reality is complicated.

Sometimes a proprietary format is simply the best choice. Sometimes compatibility matters more than ideology. Sometimes the software I need only works with a particular format. I don’t want to turn my computer into a set of arbitrary restrictions where I refuse to use something simply because it isn’t open.

That isn’t freedom either.

For me, it’s more about making a conscious choice whenever I have one.

If I can save something in an open format without losing anything important, why wouldn’t I?

Perhaps this is another reason I enjoy Linux.

Linux has always encouraged me to think a little more carefully about what is happening beneath the surface. It reminds me that computers don’t have to be sealed boxes where everything is decided for me. I can look inside. I can change things. I can choose different tools.

And I can choose, at least some of the time, how my own data is stored.

There is something quietly satisfying about that.

I don’t expect my collection of files to survive forever. Hard drives fail. Backups disappear. Formats become obsolete. Even open standards can eventually fade into history.

But I like knowing that I’m giving my files a fighting chance.

Maybe, years from now, someone will find an old folder of mine and discover a collection of simple, understandable files. Perhaps they’ll be able to open them without needing to track down some ancient piece of software or create an account with a company that hasn’t existed for decades.

Maybe they’ll just open the file.

And it will work.

That, to me, is one of the quiet beauties of open formats.

They give our data a little more independence from the software that created it.

I’m not trying to become a purist. I’m simply trying to make better choices where I can.

One file at a time.

One format at a time.

A small attempt at digital freedom.
